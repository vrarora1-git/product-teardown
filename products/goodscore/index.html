01 · The Read

What GoodScore actually is.

A score app on the surface. A secured-card acquisition funnel underneath. Once you see it this way, every other product decision starts to make sense — and the ones that don't, become the opportunity.

GoodScore is the consumer-facing layer of Arthvit 1809 Tech, the same company that operates Rupicard — an FD-backed RuPay credit card where users park a deposit (from ₹5,000) and get a credit limit at ~90% of it. The score app is top-of-funnel. The card is where the money is made.

That framing changes everything: feature priorities, growth metrics, what "success" looks like for the PM owning this. The score app's job isn't to be the best score app. Its job is to convert a curious, anxious, thin-file user into a secured-card holder — and then into a graduated, unsecured borrower.

The single most important sentence in this memo

GoodScore is not a credit-score business. It is a customer-acquisition engine for a secured-lending business. Every product gap I flag in Section 3 makes sense only if you see the app this way — and the playbook in Section 4 is built around fixing this funnel, not adding score features.

1.2The under-the-surface truth

Lesser known · pattern recognition

The SMS permission is the real product. GoodScore takes full SMS access via a foreground service. Combined with its Axis Bank TPAP UPI authorisation and CKYC/GST data permissions, the app collects far more than a score app needs. The thin-file NTC user is being assembled into an alternative-data underwriting profile in real time. The score is the consent.

This is fine — it's smart, actually. But it means the moat isn't the score; it's the behavioural dataset. The product roadmap should be optimised to deepen that dataset, not to gloss the score screen.

1.3The competitive read in one line each

  • OneScore (FPL Tech, OneCard) — bank-grade trust, free-tier strong, weak conversion to monetisation. Their funnel is the inverse of GoodScore's: under-monetised reach.
  • Paisabazaar / BankBazaar — marketplace lead-gen plays. Score is a hook to sell loans. Different unit economics, same end state.
  • CRED — not actually a competitor for NTC. CRED is for prime users. The bottom 60% of India by score is GoodScore's TAM and CRED doesn't want them.
  • Oolka — AI-first credit companion, well-designed but small. Real threat is the design quality, not the distribution.
  • OneCard, Slice, Uni (where alive) — the prime-card players. The graduation destination for a successful Rupicard customer is one of them. Either you build that next step in-house or you lose the customer at the moment of greatest LTV.

02 · Customer Problems

What users are actually saying — once you read past the 4.7★.

A 4.7-star app with a heavy review-solicitation loop hides its own data. I read the 1- and 2-star tail carefully. Three patterns repeat — and they're load-bearing for the business, not cosmetic.

01

The forced-autopay trap.

Users sign up expecting a free score check, land directly on a paywall (₹84 + GST/month with a 12-hour countdown), and discover post-payment that they've authorised a recurring autopay mandate. The refund policy is non-refundable, non-prorated. Every service failure becomes a grievance that customer support absorbs.

Pattern in reviews: "waste of money, it's paid subscription and Autopay is compulsory" · "they call me next day… so I edit my review" — the cycle of bait → frustration → support save → forced positive review repeats consistently across hundreds of 1-star reviews.
02

The Credit Expert promise that doesn't scale.

The premium tier sells a human credit expert who calls you back, helps with disputes, and gives personalised guidance. The recurring failure is the callback never happening — or happening once, then never again. The highest-value paid feature is the most operationally fragile, because it depends on a human layer that doesn't scale at the rate the funnel feeds it.

What this tells us: the service promise is set by marketing, sized by ops, and broken by physics. This is a roadmap problem, not a training problem.
03

The score credibility gap.

GoodScore shows Experian + CRIF. Users in India check CIBIL when banks evaluate them. Reviewers note their GoodScore number reads materially lower than the CIBIL number they see elsewhere — which makes the "increase to 750+" promise feel manufactured. The entire premium pitch sits on a number the user doesn't trust as their "real" score.

Pattern in reviews: "the value given in the Credit Score Section is much minimized than from the actual value present in the CIBIL SCORE REPORT" — and this surfaces in the 2- and 3-star bracket, where reviewers like the app but distrust the headline number.
My own walkthrough · iOS, paid user

I bought the subscription to see the post-paywall product. Five things stood out — all of them fixable, none of them currently fixed:

1. No login/sign-up before the paywall. The pitch isn't earned; the friction is. 2. The pre-payment pitch isn't convincing — it sells features, not the outcome the user came for. 3. Post-payment, the experience is identical across score bands. A user with a 480 score, a user with a 680 score, and a user with no bureau record get the same flow with different numbers swapped in. 4. The downloadable PDF report is functionally what CRIF gives you free — there's no insight layer that justifies the premium. 5. The "5-step plan" is the same plan for everyone.

Translation: the product stops thinking about the user the moment they pay. That is the single most expensive problem in the funnel, because it's where churn is born.

03 · Gaps & Leaks

Six leaks in the machine, ranked by what they cost.

Some of these are product. Some are business model. All of them are fixable in a quarter or two. I've ordered them by impact on the unit economics — not by how visible they are on the surface.

Leak 01 · Highest cost

The post-paywall product doesn't earn the price.

What's brokenSame experience across all score bands. Same plan, same nudges, same PDF. A user who paid ₹99 to "improve" their 720 score gets the same advice as a user with no bureau record. Premium feels like a paywall with cosmetic differences.

How I'd fix itBuild four distinct post-paywall journeys: NTC/thin-file, sub-prime (300–500), mid-prime (500–700), prime (700+). Each band gets its own next-best-action, its own coaching script, its own product offer. This isn't personalisation as a feature — it's the entire reason the user paid.

Leak 02

Acquisition spend isn't graduating into Rupicard fast enough.

What's brokenThe score app feeds the funnel; Rupicard is the monetisation. But the bridge between the two — the trigger, the offer, the moment of conversion — is currently a static banner. Most score users never get a band-appropriate, behaviourally-timed card offer.

How I'd fix itBuild the score → card conversion as a designed flow, not a banner. Three triggers: (a) NTC user completes second monthly score check → FD card offer with ₹5K minimum; (b) sub-prime user makes an on-time bill payment → "you're 60 days from card eligibility" nudge; (c) score-drop event → "secure your credit history" card pitch. This is the exact ladder I built at MobiKwik.

Leak 03

No CIBIL is a credibility ceiling.

What's brokenExperian + CRIF only. The user's lived experience is that CIBIL is "the" score. Until the app shows CIBIL alongside, every dispute about "my real score is higher" is a churn risk.

How I'd fix itEither (a) integrate CIBIL — the commercial conversation is hard but solvable, or (b) lean into bureau diversification as the feature: "Why one score isn't enough" content + side-by-side compare. Option (b) buys time, option (a) wins the war. Run (b) now, negotiate (a) in parallel.

Leak 04

The paywall screen is leaking trust faster than conversions.

What's brokenCountdown timer, struck-through ₹199, "Limited Offer applied," forced autopay, no visible total cost, no trust marks (bureau logos, RBI, user count) — all on the highest-stakes screen for a low-financial-literacy user. The screen optimises for first payment over retained user.

How I'd fix itRebuild the paywall: honest price strip ("₹99/mo incl. GST · cancel anytime"), trust band under the CTA (Experian + CRIF + RBI-authorised + 5Cr users), value ladder reordered to lead with the fear-resolution job (fraud disputes), remove the countdown. I'd run this as an A/B against the current screen — my prior is the honest version converts equal or better, and the retention curve improves by 200–400 bps.

Leak 05

The Credit Expert chat is the wrong physics.

What's brokenHuman callbacks at the scale of 5Cr users and millions of paid subscribers don't work. The best users get served. The rest churn quietly.

How I'd fix itRe-architect "Credit Expert" as AI-first, human-escalated. Tier 1: an AI coach trained on the user's actual bureau data — answers 80% of questions in seconds. Tier 2: human dispute specialist for the 20% where it matters (genuinely fraudulent accounts, complex bureau errors). This is also the right shape for unit economics: the cost-per-resolution drops by an order of magnitude while the experience improves.

Leak 06

The PDF is a missed opportunity.

What's brokenThe downloadable credit report is essentially what the bureau gives the user for free if they ask. There's no GoodScore layer of insight, score forecast, action plan, or shareability for loan applications.

How I'd fix itTurn the PDF into a GoodScore-branded "Credit Health Card" — bureau data + score simulator output + 90-day action plan + a sharable summary the user can hand to a lender. Becomes a viral artefact and a real reason to renew. Low engineering effort, high differentiation.

Pattern

Five of these six leaks share one root cause: the product is designed for the first payment, not the second. Fix the second-payment experience and you change the unit economics without touching acquisition spend. That's the thesis of Section 4.

04 · The Playbook

What I'd ship in the first quarter — and the two years it sets up.

Three Q1 phases, each with one headline outcome. Then a near-term and long-term horizon to show where this leads. No phase touches the acquisition machine; the entire plan converts paid users into retained, graduating ones.

Days 1–30
See & Stabilise

Stop the trust bleed. Understand the funnel.

Before adding anything, I want to know exactly where the funnel leaks and which users churn. Three ship-fast items, one deep-dive.

  • ShipPaywall honesty pass. Real total price visible. Trust band added (Experian + CRIF + RBI-authorised + 5Cr users). Countdown removed. A/B vs current. Hypothesis: equal or better conversion, +200–400 bps D30 retention — and it pre-empts the regulatory exposure (see callout below).
  • ShipCancel-flow rebuild. One-tap cancel + reason capture + win-back offer. Cuts refund-grievance volume and gives us churn-reason data we don't currently have.
  • ShipLogin before paywall. Today the user pays before identifying themselves. This breaks bureau-data continuity, makes attribution impossible, and is the single biggest reason post-pay personalisation isn't shipping. Capture phone + consent first, then pitch — conversion will dip ~5–8%, retention will rise sharply.
  • DigCohort funnel teardown. Map conversion by score band, acquisition channel, and device. I expect we'll find one or two cohorts paying but never returning — that's where Phase 2 lands.
Regulatory pressure point — why Phase 1 can't wait

The current paywall (forced autopay + non-refundable + countdown timer + struck-through anchor price) sits in the exact zone the DPDP Act and RBI's digital-lending direction are tightening on for financial apps serving vulnerable segments. Fixing this in Days 1–30 isn't ethics, it's regulatory cover. I've operated inside RBI card and lending compliance for the last two years — I know where this line is.

Days 31–60
Differentiate post-pay

The product starts thinking about the user after they pay.

The biggest leak is the identical post-pay experience. This phase fixes that, segment by segment, and makes the advice executable inside the app instead of being a video the user watches and then doesn't act on.

  • ShipBand-specific journeys (v1). Four distinct flows for NTC, sub-prime, mid-prime, prime. Different home screens, different next-best-actions, different coaching scripts. Personalisation as the product, not a feature.
  • ShipIn-app execution loop. Today the advice lives in a video; the action lives somewhere else. I'd close the loop: "reduce utilisation" routes to bill-pay in-app, "clear overdue" routes to the EMI repayment flow, "raise a dispute" launches a guided bureau-dispute workflow. Execution stays inside the app — which means we see the outcome, not just the intent.
  • ShipScore simulator. "If you repay ₹X early, your score will increase by Y points by [date]." Behavioural, sticky, gives every visit a reason. This is the daily-active hook.
  • ShipGoodScore Credit Health Card (PDF v2). Branded, shareable, actually useful at a lender — not the same data CRIF gives the user for free. Becomes a referral artefact and a renewal trigger.
Days 61–90
Build the graduation rail

Make Rupicard the obvious next step — not a banner.

This is where the score app starts paying for itself by feeding the card. The bridge is the business — and this is the exact ladder I scaled to 20,000 cards / ₹150Cr FD book at MobiKwik.

  • ShipScore → Card conversion flow. Three behavioural triggers (NTC second-check, sub-prime on-time payment, score-drop event) each routed to a band-appropriate Rupicard offer. Not a banner. A flow.
  • ShipAI Credit Coach (v1). Tier-1 chat trained on the user's actual bureau data — resolves 70–80% of queries in seconds. The human "Credit Expert" tier survives, but only for the disputes and complex cases where it actually adds value. Cost-per-resolution drops by an order of magnitude; service-promise reliability rises.
  • PlanCIBIL commercial conversation begins. Even if it ships in Q2, signalling the path now closes the credibility gap in the narrative we tell users.
Q2
Near term · 3–6 mo

Goals replace tasks. The product becomes a co-pilot.

Once the post-pay product is band-specific and the execution loop is closed, the next step is letting the user set the destination — and the product becoming the route to it.

  • ShipGoal-anchored plans. User declares a goal — "home loan in 6 months," "my first credit card," "lower my EMI" — and we generate a personalised plan with weekly milestones drawn from their actual bureau + transaction data. Every visit has a purpose. Every nudge has a reason. This is the move that turns a score-checker into a daily user.
  • ShipCIBIL live (target). Three-bureau view becomes default. Score-credibility leak (Leak 03) closes.
  • ShipGraduation product v1. First unsecured offer to seasoned Rupicard holders — the bridge from secured-card to credit-line, modelled on what I built at MobiKwik (8L+ active users, ₹476Cr+ monthly throughput).
H2
Long term · 6–12 mo

The moat: bank history alongside bureau data.

This is where GoodScore stops being a credit-score app and starts being something no bureau-only competitor can replicate — and where the free tier earns the right to charge for premium.

  • ShipAccount Aggregator integration. Bank history alongside bureau data — income, savings, EMI discipline, salary cadence. Advice sharpens by an order of magnitude. No bureau-only player can replicate this; it's the structural moat, and it directly deepens the alternative-data dataset I called out in Section 1.
  • ShipTiered subscription, earned. Free tier becomes trusted and sticky first; then premium follows — ₹79/mo (basic monitoring), ₹149/mo (full profile + simulator), ₹249/mo (multi-bureau + credit expert on call). We charge because we've earned it, not because we trapped the user behind a paywall. This is the unit-economics fix.
  • ShipBehavioural credit index (v1). Merging AA + bureau data into a proprietary score GoodScore owns. This is the long-term defensibility play — the data product the company eventually sells back to lenders.

Equally important · what I'd deprioritise

Senior PMs prove judgment by what they cut. Three things I'd not do in the first quarter:

1. No score-engine rebuild. The bureau data is what it is. Spending engineering on a "better" score number is a vanity project. 2. No home-screen redesign. The conversion problem is post-paywall, not pre-paywall. A redesign is the lazy answer. 3. No new acquisition channels. Distribution is solved (5Cr users). Adding more inflow to a leaky bucket makes the unit economics worse, not better. The job is to fix what happens after they arrive.
